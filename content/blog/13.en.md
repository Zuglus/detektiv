Our agency was approached by a woman who had suspected her husband of cheating for several months. He often went on business trips to another city, and although there was no direct evidence, the suspicions kept building. The couple were drifting apart: the husband had become more secretive, talked less about work and spent less time at home.

The woman understood that the truth might not be what she imagined — and still she decided to find it out.

### The First Three Days

The detectives drew up a surveillance plan and began work during the husband’s next business trip. Surveillance in another city requires preparation: you need to know in advance where the person will stay, where his meetings are and how he gets around. Arriving in his city, we discreetly followed him from the hotel to the offices where his meetings and negotiations took place. The first three days raised no suspicion: colleagues, meetings, evenings in a single room. The trip looked purely professional.

### The Fourth Day

On the fourth day, when his meetings were over, he left the hotel and took a taxi to the other end of the city — for the first time during the whole trip. A young woman was waiting for him in a small café. They had dinner and talked for a long time, and then went to her home, where he stayed until morning. The next morning he returned to the hotel as if nothing had happened.

It was not a chance affair. A few days later it all happened again, and on his next business trip he met the same young woman once more — and we recorded another meeting that clearly had nothing to do with work.

> However bitter the truth may be, only the truth gives you solid ground for further decisions.

### The Result

After the surveillance we gave our client a dossier: photos of the meetings, routes, confirmation of overnight stays. She had come to us hoping her suspicions would not be confirmed, and received a bitter truth instead. But it was this truth that helped her decide how to go on.

### If the Doubts Won’t Go Away

We understand how painful such a discovery can be. But living with constant doubt is no easier. If you feel that something is wrong, rely on facts rather than guesses: they will either dispel your suspicions or confirm them, and in either case they will give you a basis for a decision.

Facts are needed not only for peace of mind. If things are heading toward divorce, the division of property or a dispute over children, you can only rely on what has been confirmed, not on suspicions.
